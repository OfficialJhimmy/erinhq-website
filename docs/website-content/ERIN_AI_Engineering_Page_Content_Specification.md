## 10. What AI Engineering Looks Like in Practice
H2:
From model to working business system.
Intro:
A typical AI system may connect several layers. The exact architecture changes depending on the business problem, data, users and operational requirements.
1. User or business event — A person, message, document, transaction or system event starts the workflow.
1. Application layer — The product or interface captures the request and controls the user experience.
1. Orchestration — The system decides what AI task, workflow or tool should run.
1. Knowledge and data — The system retrieves the information required for the task.
1. Model layer — One or more AI models process the request within the defined context.
1. Tools and integrations — The system can call approved APIs, databases or business tools where required.
1. Validation and guardrails — Outputs are checked against rules, schemas or evaluation criteria.
1. Human review — Sensitive, ambiguous or high-impact actions can be routed to people.
1. Monitoring and evaluation — The system is measured and improved using real usage and evaluation data.

## 12. Building With the Right Level of AI
H2:
Not every problem needs an autonomous agent.
Body copy:
Good AI engineering starts with choosing the simplest architecture that can reliably solve the problem. Sometimes that is a straightforward workflow. Sometimes it is a retrieval system. Sometimes an agent is useful. The architecture should follow the problem, not the trend.
Show three levels:
- AI-assisted workflow — AI supports a defined step inside an existing process.
- AI-powered workflow — AI handles multiple connected steps with business rules and integrations.
- Agentic system — AI can plan, use tools and execute a defined multi-step task within controlled boundaries.
Closing line:
The goal is not to make a system look intelligent. The goal is to make the workflow work better.

## 17. AI Engineering Process
H2:
A practical process for turning an AI idea into a working system.
1. Discovery — Understand the business problem, users, workflow, data and constraints.
1. AI feasibility — Determine whether AI is actually useful and where it should sit in the workflow.
1. Architecture — Define the system components, data flows, models, integrations and human controls.
1. Prototype — Build the smallest useful version to validate the core behaviour.
1. Evaluation — Test quality, reliability, failure modes, cost and user experience.
1. Production build — Engineer the full application, integrations and infrastructure.
1. Deployment — Launch the agreed system and establish monitoring.
1. Iteration — Improve the system using feedback, evaluation and real-world usage.

## 23. Suggested FAQ Section
What is AI engineering?
AI engineering is the work of designing and building complete applications and systems around AI models. That can include data, retrieval, agents, APIs, software, infrastructure, evaluation and human workflows.
Do you only build AI agents?
No. The architecture depends on the problem. Some workflows are best solved with traditional software, some with AI-assisted workflows, some with retrieval systems and some with controlled agentic systems.
Can you integrate AI into our existing software?
Yes. AI can be designed as part of an existing product, internal tool or business workflow where the available systems and APIs support the integration.
Can you build a custom AI system?
Yes. The AI Solutions catalogue provides examples of common systems, but custom systems can be designed around a specific organisation's workflow and requirements.
Do you work with companies outside Nigeria?
Yes. ERIN is based in Lagos, Nigeria and works with businesses and organisations globally through remote collaboration.
How do you make AI systems reliable?
Reliability depends on the system. Common engineering practices include grounded retrieval, structured outputs, validation, evaluation, monitoring, controlled tool access and human escalation.

## 25. UX Requirements
- The first screen must clearly communicate AI engineering rather than generic software development.
- Use architecture diagrams and system visuals to explain complex concepts.
- Technical detail should progressively deepen as the visitor scrolls.
- Use expandable details where appropriate instead of large walls of text.
- Technical terms should be accompanied by plain-language explanations.
- Use real project visuals whenever possible.
- Avoid fake dashboards or fabricated metrics.
- Keep the page fast and accessible.
- Use semantic HTML and a clear H1/H2 hierarchy.
- All interactive diagrams must have an accessible text equivalent.
- Respect prefers-reduced-motion.
- The page should work equally well for a CTO evaluating technical capability and a business leader evaluating whether ERIN can solve a problem.
## 26. Content Guardrails
- Do not position AI engineering as prompt writing alone.
- Do not use 'AI-powered' as empty marketing language. Explain what the AI actually does.
- Do not claim production deployment, scale, reliability or performance without evidence.
- Do not invent client names, logos, results or case-study metrics.
- Do not imply that an AI model can make sensitive decisions without appropriate human oversight.
- Do not force an agentic architecture where a simpler workflow is more appropriate.
- Keep Nigeria as the home base, not the limit of the market.
- Use British English.
- Never start a sentence with 'And'.
- Avoid em dashes and en dashes throughout the website.
